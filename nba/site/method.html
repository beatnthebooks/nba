Method and research

The NBA edition runs the same tests as the NFL site: a rating model against the betting market, every idea checked on seasons it never saw. The short version: the NBA closing line is even harder to beat than the NFL’s. Our model lost to it in all seven test seasons, and none of the 47 signals we tried held up. What the site offers instead is one place to see every game, the fair price, and where Kalshi or Polymarket is cheaper than it.

1. The market price

A moneyline becomes a probability with q = 1 / d, where d is decimal odds. Both sides add up to more than 100%; the extra is the book’s margin. Removing it gives the fair price:

pfair = qhome / (qhome + qaway)

For finished games the site uses the closing line: the middle (median) of every sportsbook ESPN lists for that game, usually 5 to 12 books. For upcoming games ESPN shows DraftKings’ current line, and the fair price is the middle of three prices: DraftKings with its margin removed, Kalshi’s midpoint and Polymarket’s midpoint. One sportsbook alone is a shaky fair price; in the NFL test the two exchanges were within about 1 win-percentage point of the sportsbooks’ final price.

Expected value always uses the price you’d actually get, fees included: expected return = p · d − 1. Kalshi charges about 7% × price × (1 − price) per contract, Polymarket 5%.

2. The Elo model

Every team has a rating, updated after every game since 2008–09 with FiveThirtyEight’s margin-of-victory multiplier. Each summer, ratings keep 75% of their distance from average. Settings were tuned on game results from 2009–10 to 2018–19 only (no betting lines): K = 18, and a team on the second night of a back-to-back loses 30 Elo (about 1 point). Rest beyond that is worth 3 Elo per day, capped at 3 days.

Phome = 1 / (1 + 10−Δ/400), Δ = Rhome + HFA + adj − Raway

Home court isn’t fixed. It fell across the league, from about 3.5 points before 2020 to closer to 2 since, so HFA is the league’s average home margin over the last two seasons plus this one so far: points right now (28 Elo per point). Neutral-court games (Mexico City, Europe, the NBA Cup final) get none.

What the model can’t see: who is playing. In the NBA one star sitting out moves a line 5 to 10 points, and teams rest players on purpose. The market knows the injury report the minute it changes; our Elo doesn’t.

That’s the honest number the site uses, so “model leans” almost never appear, and model picks (a model edge of 5% or more) essentially never. They’re still computed and graded, so the Season page shows what they would have done.

4. Looking for an edge against the market

Each idea is tested as something the market might have missed: given the closing price, does it still predict the result?

logit P(win) = b0 + b1 · logit(pmarket) + b2 · signal

If b2 is zero, the market already prices the signal. The rule was fixed before any test number was seen: a signal had to be clearly non-zero (|z| ≥ 2) on 2013–14 to 2018–19 and point the same way in both halves of that period. Then 2019–20 to 2025–26 were scored once, each season fit on earlier seasons only, betting at the closing price.

Tested on the moneyline and the spread (17 each): back-to-backs, rest difference, 3 games in 4 nights, time zones crossed, altitude (Denver, Utah), road-trip length, last game’s margin, last game against the spread, season against the spread, win/loss streak, home underdog, big underdogs, Elo vs the market (all season and the first 10 games), playoffs, moneyline vs spread, a points-based rating. On the total (13): back-to-backs, rest, altitude, total line vs league average, teams’ season over/under, last game over/under, a points-based total, spread size, early season, playoffs, time zones, overtime last game, 3 in 4 nights.

All three looked real on the older seasons and disappeared on the newer ones: the market was as accurate or more accurate in every case. Back-to-backs, the most talked-about NBA angle, didn’t even pass the first screen: the line already moves for them. Nothing was adopted. Blind baselines over the same test seasons at the closing price: every home moneyline −5.3%, every away −2.8%, every home spread −5.0%, every away −4.1%, every over −3.4%, every under −5.7%.

5. What the “Bets to take” boxes suggest

Price gap · unproven Kalshi or Polymarket selling a side at least 2% cheaper than the fair price, after fees. In the NFL test, gaps like this an hour before kickoff returned +40% to +50% on 39 bets, which is promising but could be luck (t = 1.0). There’s no NBA test of them yet. Sized at 0.5 units, 0.75 if our model agrees. Gaps on totals and spreads only count at the sportsbook’s own line.

Model pick · no proven edge Our model’s blended edge of 5% or more on the moneyline or the spread, shown with its real record. With the model at 1% weight these should be rare to nonexistent. Sized at 0.25 units.

Every suggestion is saved the first time it appears and graded at that price, including whether it beat the closing line. Beating the close more than half the time is the earliest sign that an edge is real. The Season page keeps the score.

6. What would actually help

The gap between our model and the market is mostly lineups. The next upgrade would value each player (minutes and impact from box scores) and adjust for who’s out on the injury report, the way the NFL model adjusts for quarterbacks. That has to be tested the same way before it earns a place on the board.
